Australia's Threatened Species

The Australian Government's Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment, and Water (DCCEW) published the Threatened Species Action Plan (TSAP) 2022-2032 in 2022, detailling their strategies for the recovery of a number of threatened species across Australia. This map visualises areas where these species, known as priority species, had been observed or were known to be likely to occur as of 2022.

Note that certain areas are spread across Australia- click on one to see what parts of Australia it covers. It can be observed that threatened species are more diverse along coastlines. There are less in the centre of Australia, which makes sense given the harsher climate.

The Threatened Species Index (TSX) is a TERN project combining monitoring data from over 230 different sources. This map shows the centres of IBRA subregions where monitoring sites were located- these regions are classified based on common factors like climate, geography, and vegetation.

Each monitoring site records data for one threatened species, these species can be classified by taxonomic group into Amphibians, Birds, Mammals and Plants. It can be seen that Birds are the most common taxonomic group to be monitored, with Mammals as the second most common and Amphibians and Plants roughly equal.

The heatmap above shows how often the most common 20 TSAP species were observed together. White squares indicate when species never occurred together. Along the diagonal, grey squares count how many total areas a species was observed in- hover over them to see the exact value.

Interestingly, Australian Bitterns observed in at least 1 area with every other one of the most common 20 species. On the other side of the scale, Carnaby's Black Cockatoo was rarely observed with the other species here. It is quite possible that it co-occurred with species that were not among the 20 most common.

The TSX collates records of abundance for each species to determine the change in their population over time. Data for each year represents the change in population since the last year, with the reference value 1 being the abundance in the first recorded year, 1985.

It can be observed that Amphibians experienced the most rapid decline in population. While the other taxonomic groups largely only declined in population, Mammals actually experienced some reasonable increases in the early year. Despite this, populations of all species reduced by as much as 40% of their original values by 2021.

Australia's States and Territories seem to be managing their endangered species roughly equally effectively. While the Australian Capital Territory has a very small percentage of Critically Endangered species, this is likely due to its small size meaning that fewer species are at risk there. Of the other States and Territories, the Northern Territory stands out with only 3.73% of species Critically Endangered- its larger size means this is likely indicative of good management strategies.

It can be seen above that Amphibians have by far the greatest proportion of Critically Endangered species. This aligns with the above time series, which shows the rapid decline in Amphibian abundance. The other taxonomic groups have roughly equivalent proportions of each Endangered status (with a few exceptions).

Notably, even though the TSX map shows that there are a significant number of monitoring sites for Birds, approximately 60% of them were only Near Threatened or Vulnerable.

A wide variety of survey types were performed across the monitoring sites of the TSX. Some of the Other approaches included 'Live trapping', 'Fixed route search' and 'Total count'. Given that the TSX collates information across sources, this variety makes sense as different sources would have different naming conventions.

Notably, three of the the eight most common survey types were specifically for birds- Shorebird count area survey, Roost count, and Individual roost surveys. This aligns with the significant number of monitoring sites for birds, as seen on the TSX map.

Counts above 200 were clipped to 200 here, as this still allowed 89.7% of values to be shown properly, and prevented compression from outliers in the thousands. It can be seen that National Priority species did not necessarily have more monitoring sites dedicated to them, nor did Critically Endangered species.

It is important to note that number of monitoring sites does not necessarily indicate that a certain type of species are being managed more effectively or thoroughly. Intangibles, like what sort of action was taken based off a certain site's results, are not recorded in this data. The number of sites alone does not entirely define effort put into managing species.
